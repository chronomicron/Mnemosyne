# Brainstorming Ideas

An open list of possibilities for Mnemosyne. The project may branch into several independent apps, devices, or services; it does not need to become one all-in-one product. Add freely, including rough, overlapping, ambitious, or far-fetched ideas. Nothing here is a decision, product plan, or claim that an idea will work. We will see what sticks.

## Project ethos to preserve

- Treat security as a first-order requirement in every version, from the earliest prototype onward. Protect the resident's identity, family details, voice, routines, and any other personal information.
- Distinguish app-code security from conversation-library security. The app must be trustworthy and protected from tampering, while the personalized scripts are sensitive resident data; protecting the scripts from exposure, unauthorized access, or loss is a particularly important priority.
- Make the project genuinely helpful to people living with Alzheimer's or dementia and their families; do not take advantage of their needs or vulnerability.
- Mnemosyne's primary motivation is helping people living with Alzheimer's or dementia in daily life, including people with severe dementia who may need frequent reassurance and interaction in a care residence. The ideas are grounded in the family's lived experience; they are not limited to occasional memory lapses. Each person's needs and responses can still differ.
- A trusted family member, caregiver, or social worker may handle technical setup and administration, but do not assume the resident cannot participate simply because they have dementia. Make it possible to include their preferences, assent, and choices to the extent they can and want to participate.
- The setup role should belong to a person the resident trusts or an appropriately authorized representative, not automatically to any relative. What formal authority a family member or proxy has depends on the role and applicable local rules; the app should not assume a family relationship grants blanket authority over every choice.
- The initial use case assumes a trusted caregiver or responsible administrator has access to set up the resident's phone or home system, create the profile, choose what information is collected, configure schedules and security, and test the experience. Downloading the app alone should not grant access to a resident profile, monitoring settings, or caregiver reports; require an authenticated administrator and deliberate device pairing before setup is activated.
- Consider stronger administrator setup security: a verified caregiver account, passkey or strong authentication, a one-time pairing code between the caregiver's computer and the resident's device, and clear control over who can view reports or change monitoring settings. On ordinary consumer phones, the app may not be able to prevent someone from downloading it through the operating system's app store, so secure enrollment and administrator access are the practical controls to design first.
- Treat covert partner tracking as a primary abuse case to prevent: for example, a boyfriend/stalker creates an anonymous account and secretly enrolls a girlfriend's phone after getting temporary access to it. Require a caregiver account tied to a verified mobile number to add friction and accountability, then require deliberate pairing with the resident's device. Do not provide stealth installation, hidden-app, disguised-icon, or silent enrollment modes. Show the resident a clear explanation of background location and who receives it, make active sharing and connected caregiver accounts visible on the resident's device, and notify the resident of new pairing, location access, or responder-sharing changes; provide a way to review, pause, or revoke access where appropriate.
- Add a gentle, voice-first transparency check-in during setup, after access changes, and at a caregiver-configurable interval: say who (by name/relationship) can see the resident's location, what they can see, and the safety purpose. For example: “Your daughter Anna can see your recent location in KinBridge in case you need help getting home. Would you like me to explain that or call Anna?” This can use the companion's call-style ring-and-answer audio interaction so the resident need not read a pop-up; make clear it is KinBridge speaking, not a real call from Anna. Keep wording clear and non-leading; allow the resident to ask who can see the information, hear the explanation again, or give feedback.
- Require caregiver account registration to verify a phone number with a one-time code by SMS or automated voice call. This adds friction and a contact channel to discourage casual covert setup, but proves only that the registrant can receive a code at that number—not their real-world identity, relationship to the resident, or authority to monitor them. Pair it with strong account authentication, deliberate resident-device pairing, and clear on-device disclosure; offer an accessible alternative when phone verification is unavailable and account for number changes, SIM swaps, and number porting.
- Where the resident cannot complete setup independently, explore a documented caregiver/authorized-representative setup path that still requires strong caregiver authentication, a second-device pairing check, and a visible record on the resident's phone. Do not treat possession of an unlocked phone as sufficient proof that someone is entitled to monitor its owner. Design the disclosure and controls so they work for the resident's cognitive and accessibility needs.
- Separate caregiver/admin controls from conversation requests. Protected settings such as privacy, data sharing, approved contacts, schedule boundaries, and family-set safety or topic restrictions should not be changed by instructions spoken during an AI conversation; in a cloud version, enforce these rules in the app's protected configuration, not only in a model prompt.
- The conversation must not expose administrator secrets or privileged access in response to spoken requests: for example, it should not reveal Wi-Fi credentials, change protected settings, or unlock a smart door because the resident asks it to override its instructions.
- Keep conversational AI separate from device-control privileges. The companion should not have access to credentials or high-impact smart-home actions it does not need; if future integrations are explored, use explicit allow-lists and separate authentication for sensitive actions such as locks.
- Preserve the resident's immediate control of participation: they can decline a call, pause, skip a topic, or end the conversation. Caregiver settings should guide the service without forcing someone to keep talking.
- Keep setup and everyday use as simple as possible for the resident and the family.
- Distinguish the person who sets up or pays for the service from the person it serves. The likely setup audience is an adult child or other family caregiver, a care worker, social worker, or senior-residence organization; the resident is the intended beneficiary and may use the companion without being the person who installs or manages it.
- Frame the main product as a caregiver app/service, not an app marketed to a person with dementia. The caregiver is the primary app user who installs it and configures schedules, reminders, routines, reports, and privacy settings; the senior uses the companion/helper experience provided by that caregiver-facing service.
- Keep the central purpose of Mnemosyne's caregiver-facing tools explicit: **help the helpers**—the *proches aidants* and other people supporting someone who lives with dementia or Alzheimer's. KinBridge and its related tools should help them organize care, support day-to-day life, stay connected, and make sense of selected observations over time; resident-facing experiences are part of that support, not the sole product audience.
- Treat caregiver well-being and workload as a first-class design goal. Reduce the helper's memory and coordination burden with a clear care calendar, refill and appointment follow-ups, organized routines, understandable updates, and optional coordination across family members or care workers. The service should support the helper as a person, not only collect information about the resident.
- Keep KinBridge's core audience and reason for use explicit: caregivers supporting an aging parent whose memory or other cognitive abilities are becoming difficult enough to affect daily routines, check-ins, or independent safety. This may include a parent with suspected or diagnosed dementia or Alzheimer's. Explain how KinBridge helps with practical routines, companionship, selected activity signals, and caregiver updates; do not imply the app can diagnose dementia or determine its cause or severity.
- Make the resident-facing part feel friendly and respectful: use gentle, clear language, simple choices, readable controls, and spoken prompts that are easy for a senior to understand. Make voice and audible cues (such as a spoken reminder, familiar ring, or bell) the primary way to deliver interactions, reminders, and alerts; text or pop-up messages can supplement them but should not be required.
- Treat audio as the main conversational input and output: the resident speaks, speech recognition may convert the audio to text for the app to interpret, and the app responds aloud using text-to-speech. Any transcript can be an internal processing step; the resident does not need to read or type it. Explore on-device speech recognition for local/offline use and cloud recognition as an optional path.
- Aim product marketing and the purchase decision at caregivers and care organizations—not at the resident by labeling them as cognitively impaired. Speak directly to caregivers who are noticing cognitive changes and need help supporting familiar routines, staying connected, and understanding selected day-to-day signals. Present the resident-facing app simply as a friendly helper, and describe reports as observations that may help the caregiver decide when to check in or raise a concern with a professional, not as a cognitive assessment or diagnosis.
- A possible Japanese-speaking audience includes adult children who work and live far from an aging parent—for example, a son in Tokyo whose parent remains in a longtime home in a smaller town or rural area. The parent's phone could provide resident-facing voice check-ins and reminders, while sending the child a morning activity ping and weekly caregiver report to help them stay informed and follow up when useful.
- Make the caregiver-facing experience valuable to people who cannot visit every day, including relatives who live in another city: simple setup, confidence that approved companionship is available, and optional weekly routine/activity updates can help them stay connected.
- Product framing to explore: market the service to the caregiver. The resident-facing phone app provides useful check-ins, weather-aware conversation, and reminders while gathering selected routine and activity information; the main caregiver-facing value is timely activity notifications plus a weekly report that brings this information together. The caregiver gets an at-a-glance view of what appears to have happened, what was not recorded, and useful changes over the week, including when they live too far away to visit every day.
- Design for both audiences: provide clear profile, schedule, privacy, and summary controls for the setup person, while keeping the resident-facing interaction simple, friendly, and easy to start or stop.
- Prefer local, offline operation and people's own hardware where practical, with privacy and user control built in.
- Collect and retain as little sensitive data as possible; explore encrypted local storage, limited and explicit family/caregiver access, no conversation recording by default, and clear ways to review, export, or delete stored information.
- Explore two privacy tiers: strong protection from the outside world, and an explicit trusted-administrator role for the family member who sets up the system. That administrator can access the records the app actually collects and manage the resident's profile, scripts, schedule, and privacy settings.
- Make the administrator relationship transparent during setup, protect administrator access with appropriate authentication, and make clear which records exist (such as call history, preference notes, or optional transcripts). Admin access to collected data does not require collecting full audio or transcripts by default.
- Consider security across the whole device and service, including setup, updates, recovery if a phone is lost, and any optional cloud connection; simplicity should not depend on leaving personal information exposed.
- Treat the entire conversation-content pipeline as sensitive: family details entered into a script-generation tool, generated scripts, the file or method used to transfer them, and the schedule and library stored in the phone app.
- Keep public project material and private resident data in separate places: generic documentation and code may live in the GitHub repository, while real names, family histories, conversation profiles, recordings, and other identifying care information stay in local, unpushed storage. Exclude local private files from Git and review staged changes before any push so sensitive material cannot be published accidentally.
- Names, dates, relationships, and memories in scripts are private information even when used for friendly conversation. Include only details that are useful to the experience, and never put passwords, account recovery answers (such as a mother's maiden name), or other authentication secrets into a conversation profile.
- When using an external AI tool to draft scripts, consider using placeholders or minimizing identifying details, then adding personal names and details on the resident's device. Families should review generated scripts before use.
- Protect the chat library itself, since even offline Markdown or JSON scripts may contain names, family relationships, hometowns, memories, and other personal details. Explore secure import/export and storage, and ensure imported scripts are treated as content rather than executable code.
- Treat precise location histories, home and favorite-place coordinates, travel corridors, and future schedules as especially sensitive: together they could reveal where the resident is and when the home may be empty. The intended access should be limited to the resident, specifically authorized caregiver(s), and—during an appropriately authorized missing-person emergency—designated responders. Do not expose this data to unrelated users, advertising/analytics SDKs, AI model providers, or public notifications.
- Make end-to-end encryption the goal for any sensitive data shared between the resident's and caregiver's devices: encrypt it on the sender's device, keep decryption keys only on authorized devices, and let any relay server handle ciphertext it cannot read. Transport encryption such as HTTPS/TLS is still needed, but alone it only protects data in transit to the server; the server may be able to read data after receiving it unless application-level end-to-end encryption is added. Plan secure device pairing, key verification, adding/revoking caregiver devices, and recovery if a device or key is lost; recovery choices affect whether anyone else could restore access.
- Prefer calculating route deviations on the resident's device and retaining detailed location history there only as long as needed. For remote caregiver alerts, send the smallest useful update; keep exact location and routine details out of lock-screen push previews and ordinary email. If an optional cloud service relays alerts, it should not receive the content in readable form where feasible; secure caregiver authentication, least-privilege sharing, access logs, and revocation are part of the design.
- Aim to prevent unauthorized access, not promise that hacking is impossible. End-to-end encryption cannot protect data displayed on a compromised or unlocked device, a stolen caregiver account, vulnerable software, or a poorly designed recovery process. Combine it with device-level protections, strong authentication, timely updates, minimized collection/retention, and security review. Use a mobile security standard such as OWASP MASVS as a later design/review checklist; do not treat encryption alone as sufficient.
- Security for location and routine data should cover encrypted storage and transfer, key management, authentication/authorization, and prevention of accidental leaks through logs, backups, caches, analytics, or notification previews. Use a mobile security standard such as OWASP MASVS as a later design/review checklist; do not treat encryption alone as sufficient.
### Working three-tier payment model

The current direction is to have at least three tiers across the Mnemosyne suite. Exact prices, limits, and billing details remain undecided.

1. **MindSteps — Free:** Keep the senior-facing games and exercises free, including a useful personal history and progress view over time. A caregiver should be able to use MindSteps to follow activity results without needing to buy KinBridge. The exact sharing/export features included in this tier remain to be defined.
2. **KinBridge — Family/Caregiver paid plan:** A caregiver using KinBridge to support or monitor a parent from afar would pay for the service. Decide between a one-time payment and a modest recurring fee after defining the features, any server or hosting costs, and the ongoing support required.
3. **Mnemosyne Care Team — Institution plan:** A social worker, nurse, or care organization would pay for the desktop dashboard and its multi-resident caseload tools. The pricing unit (for example, per organization, staff member, or resident) and caseload limits are open questions.

Keep MindSteps' core games and personal tracking free when shaping the paid tiers. Make any paid features and data-sharing limits clear before enrollment, and make caregiver service costs transparent. Optional cloud conversations, remote reports, and family setup tools could be included in KinBridge depending on the eventual service design.

## Separate Mnemosyne apps with an optional shared caregiver view

- Keep **KinBridge** (caregiver support, routines, activity signals, and reports) and **MindSteps** (senior-facing games and cognitive exercises) as separate products under the Mnemosyne umbrella. Each should be useful on its own; a family can choose to install and use either one or both.
- Use the working brand pattern **“MindSteps from Mnemosyne”** and **“KinBridge from Mnemosyne”** so the two apps have distinct names while clearly belonging to the same project.
- Make their data compatible so, when a family uses both, authorized caregiver tools can interpret and present the records together. KinBridge focuses on day-to-day routines, activities, and device/location signals; the cognitive-exercise app focuses on periodic check-ins (for example, every few months) and performance trends across task areas. Compatibility does not require merging the apps or their databases.
- Define a shared, versioned data format for records that cross between apps: a resident/profile identifier; event and session timestamps; originating app and record type; task or signal definition; units and result values; task/content version; language and interaction mode; and relevant conditions or confidence/provenance. Provide a documented local export/import or authenticated API so records remain interpretable across app versions and can be selectively shared.
- If the family chooses to connect them, the caregiver may view selected exercise trends alongside KinBridge's day-to-day activity and routine summaries in one caregiver account or portal. Linking and sharing exercise results must be explicit, revocable, and limited to the caregiver(s) authorized for that resident.
- Keep the sources and meanings distinct in any combined report: routine/device signals in one section, and game results by skill area (such as memory, attention, language, or reaction time) in another. Show dates, task/version, and relevant context; never present a game trend as a diagnosis or as proof that a real-world activity occurred, or combine unlike records into a single unexplained “mental state” score.
- Preserve independent operation and local-first use where possible. If the memory app is used without KinBridge or without report sharing enabled, its game history remains within that app/device and is not uploaded to the caregiver service by default.

### Three-part Mnemosyne suite: typical use case

- **Senior on a tablet — MindSteps from Mnemosyne:** The senior plays approachable games and exercises. The app records results over time, including measures such as accuracy, response time, retries, and help used, organized by activity and skill area.
- **Primary caregiver on a phone — KinBridge from Mnemosyne:** A family caregiver, such as an adult son supporting his mother, uses KinBridge on their phone to stay connected, coordinate support, and review selected updates and MindSteps progress when the senior or authorized representative has enabled sharing.
- **Social worker or nurse on a computer — Mnemosyne desktop app:** A care-facility professional uses a desktop application to review and manage the residents assigned to them, potentially a caseload of around a dozen seniors. Each resident's information remains distinct, and the professional sees only their authorized caseload.
- Treat these as three connected user experiences in the Mnemosyne suite: the senior's tablet, the caregiver's phone, and the care professional's desktop. MindSteps activity records can be packaged or synchronized for authorized review in KinBridge and the desktop app, with the source app, dates, activity versions, and measures retained.

## Illustrative primary use case: adult child supporting a parent with cognitive decline

1. **Caregiver creates an account and pairs the devices.** The caregiver verifies a mobile number, secures their account, and deliberately pairs their phone or desktop with the parent's phone. The parent receives a clear spoken explanation of what the app does, which caregiver can see which information, and how to ask for help or review sharing.
2. **Caregiver and parent set up useful routines.** A guided interview records the parent's preferred name and language, familiar activities and destinations, regular schedule, reminder wording, topics to avoid, and who may receive updates. Location monitoring and emergency responder sharing are explicit choices; the caregiver maps usual places and travel corridors if those features are enabled.
3. **The parent uses a simple voice-first helper.** At agreed times, the phone speaks a gentle greeting, weather-aware prompt, planned activity reminder, or short conversation. The parent can answer, ask for help, snooze, skip, or end the interaction. Approved offline prompts continue if internet access is unavailable.
4. **The app records a modest event timeline.** It can record what it prompted and selected device signals, such as first phone motion, unplugging from power, a location ping, or a Wi-Fi change. Each item is labeled by source; a reminder is not proof that medicine was taken, and a location pattern is not proof of a specific outing.
5. **The caregiver gets useful updates.** A morning ping or weekly report summarizes selected events and routines, showing what was observed and what remains uncertain. If the phone appears outside familiar places/routes, the caregiver gets an alert and can decide whether to call. If the pattern continues and the caregiver does not acknowledge the alert, a configured voice check-in can ask whether the parent needs help.
6. **If the parent is reported missing, an emergency path can share limited location data.** If enabled and properly authorized, responders can receive a small recent slice of location pings (for example, the last five or six points or a bounded 24-hour window), rather than the full profile, routines, conversation scripts, or caregiver history. Any broader sharing remains subject to the applicable process.
7. **One caregiver account can hold a separate profile for each parent.** The caregiver creates a mother profile and a father profile, then pairs each profile with the corresponding parent's phone. Each profile has its own locations, favorite destinations, usual travel corridors, routines, reminders, privacy/responder-sharing settings, activity timeline, alerts, and reports; information must not be mixed between parents.

## Conversation companion: friendly company and conversation

- A voice companion that makes or receives a short phone call and asks how the person is doing.
- An Android phone that rings as if an incoming call is arriving, then answers with a warm, light conversation: “Hello, Agnes. I was calling to see how you're doing today.” The voice could be synthesized, and the experience could feel like a simple familiar phone call rather than a complicated app.
- A family setup flow for a simple Wi-Fi-only “companion phone” that does not need a cellular plan. A family member could enter the resident's name, age, hometown, family members, friends, pets, favorite subjects, and other background details for the conversation system to draw on.
- A narrow voice-chat app: speech recognition turns what the resident says into text, a conversation engine uses that plus the family profile to form a response, and speech synthesis reads the response aloud. The recognized text may be processed temporarily without being kept as a transcript. The app could keep each turn short and listen again, rather than trying to be a general-purpose assistant.
- A first app iteration that runs entirely on the phone and uses prewritten conversation trees rather than a live AI model. During setup, a family member could provide background details and use a tool such as ChatGPT to draft many short, roughly five-minute conversations; after review, those scripts are loaded into the app and selected for scheduled calls.
- Scripted calls could use familiar details (for example, grandchildren, pets, hometown, interests, or weather) and end naturally with a kind sign-off. Text-to-speech speaks each line; speech recognition could capture replies and use a small set of simple branches, or the script could give a warm generic response and move to the next prompt.
- A fully on-phone, offline version with no backend or cloud service during use: the app stores the reviewed scripts and schedule locally, speaks each line, and uses only voice activity detection to notice when the resident starts speaking and when they have paused. It does not need to transcribe or understand their words; after a sufficiently long pause it can continue the script with a gentle response or next question.
- One shared, prewritten conversation library and script flow that could run in different forms: as an Android app, an iPhone app, or on a Raspberry Pi inside a rotary phone. Each device would supply its own ring, microphone, speaker, and local speech handling while using the same prepared conversation content.
- In a Raspberry Pi rotary phone version, the device could ring on its own schedule; picking up the handset starts the same scripted conversation, with text-to-speech for the companion and either speech/pause detection or speech-to-text for handling the resident's turn. Hanging up ends the call.
- Turn-taking rules that distinguish a brief thinking pause from a likely end of turn, wait patiently, and avoid speaking over the resident. The app could offer an easy way to repeat or skip a prompt and use a soft, generic acknowledgment when it cannot tell whether the person has finished.
- A target conversation length (for example, around five minutes) with a graceful closing at a turn boundary, rather than abruptly stopping while the resident is speaking. The resident should still be able to continue, end early, or ignore a scheduled call.
- A library of reusable conversation patterns with family-specific names and details filled in at runtime, as an alternative to hand-authoring hundreds of complete conversations.
- A design question for scripted conversations: how should the phone respond when the resident says something the script does not expect, without sounding corrective, repetitive, or as if it understood more than it did?
- Review generated scripts before use, and consider using fictionalized or minimized personal details when drafting them with an online AI service; the family profile contains sensitive information.
- A smartphone-first version where the app stores its schedule and personal conversation profile on the phone, rings or shows a gentle prompt itself, and starts the voice chat without needing a computer in the home.
- An optional home-computer version where a laptop or small computer in the home stores the profile and runs the conversation model; the phone acts as the microphone, speaker, and simple interface over Wi-Fi. This would reduce dependence on an outside AI service but add installation and home-network setup.
- A hybrid version where the phone handles reminders, microphone, speech recognition, and voice playback, while a home computer supplies the conversation engine only during a call.
- A companion that uses this family-provided background to ask personal, familiar questions—about the children, grandchildren, a pet, the weather, a hometown, or a favorite interest—then follows the resident's lead instead of reading from a fixed script.
- Scheduled outgoing calls at a chosen rhythm. An hourly call is one possible experiment, alongside less frequent or family-triggered calls; the device could give a gentle ring, let the person ignore it, and make it easy to pause or change the schedule.
- A scheduled scripted conversation could be presented as an incoming-call-like event: the phone plays a familiar ring, then the resident answers using a large, simple on-screen control or supported call-answer action. The companion greets them aloud with text-to-speech, listens for spoken replies during the active conversation, and speaks back; the interaction should not depend on the resident reading text. This is an app-generated companion session, not necessarily a cellular call. On Android, investigate whether to use a scheduled alarm/high-priority notification or, for a more authentic calling interface, Android Telecom/VoIP integration; full-screen call-style presentation is subject to platform permissions and distribution rules.
- Keep the resident-facing experience primarily vocal rather than text-based. Let the caregiver choose a friendly companion name and voice, then use natural spoken openings—for example, “Hi Mary, it's George. Looks like it's going to be a nice day. Would you like to go to the park?” The screen can support answering and simple controls, but reading a text message should not be required.
- On-demand conversation alongside scheduled calls: a large, simple “Talk now” or “Can we talk?” button lets the resident start a friendly conversation whenever they feel lonely, rather than waiting for the next scheduled ring. It could open an available scripted or cloud-enabled conversation immediately; the button should be easy to find and use.
- Family controls for which people, pets, events, and memories are okay to mention, and which subjects should be avoided. A pet or relative who has died might be a comforting topic for one person and painful or confusing for another, so the family should be able to set preferences and revise them.
- A question about current local weather or another simple topic to make the call feel connected to the day, mixed with personalized topics supplied by family.
- A family-inspired conversational style that feels warm and familiar. The companion's identity and framing are open design choices: possibilities range from a clearly introduced companion to a familiar friend-like persona configured for the individual.
- A scheduled or family-triggered friendly call for a person who may feel anxious or alone when nobody is nearby. The starting hypothesis is that a gentle call could offer comfort and a sense of company; this should be explored with each person, since preferences will differ.
- Distinguish occasional social calls from a more frequent reassurance mode for someone whose dementia causes substantial day-to-day support needs and repeated anxiety about being alone. That mode could use predictable, brief check-ins, warm reassurance, and gentle present-day topics such as weather, music, or familiar routines.
- In reassurance mode, family could configure calming responses and topics to avoid, including painful bereavement details. Reassurance can be repeated and personalized to the person's immediate emotional needs; the goal is comfort and engagement, not testing factual recall.
- A call that stays light and responsive: ask how the person is, chat about a familiar topic, listen without quizzing, and end kindly when they are ready.
- A gentle chat about familiar people, interests, memories, or the day. For example: “How are you today?” or “How is Aunt Margaret?” when that context has been provided.
- A **conversational avatar** for a tablet: a friendly face, voice, and simple controls for face-to-face conversation.
- A central use case for the conversation companion is a resident in a care residence with severe Alzheimer's or dementia who repeatedly calls for passing staff members or asks for small things as a way to get attention, reassurance, and interaction. The person may not have a specific practical need each time; a friendly response and someone engaging with them can matter in itself.
- A response pattern for these moments could be: acknowledge the request, offer a calm, family- or care-team-approved reassurance, then gently shift the conversation to a familiar topic or activity. For example, after a recent bathroom visit, the avatar might respond warmly and invite the resident to talk about a favorite song, a meal, or something familiar, rather than repeatedly correcting them or leaving them without interaction.
- A facility-oriented, always-available reassurance companion on a large tablet, intended for residents with severe dementia who repeatedly seek interaction or reassurance. A friendly animated face with eyes, mouth, and lip-synced speech could offer the sense of talking with someone, rather than reading text from a screen.
- The avatar would act like a gentle friend and carry on low-stakes conversation about familiar people, weather, food, family, and daily routines. It could offer warm, repetitive reassurance and keep the resident engaged in the moment; it is not meant to quiz the resident or test memory.
- A family member or care-home staff member could enter background details such as names, places, jobs, interests, and family history so the conversation feels familiar. The tablet could support open-ended voice conversation, similar to the phone companion idea, but with a visible face.
- The tablet camera could help the system notice when the resident is present, looking toward the screen, or starting to interact, so the avatar can greet them and respond. A cloud-enabled version could provide a more flexible conversation; camera and profile data remain sensitive and security is a priority.
- This is a separate branch from scheduled phone calls: an always-available, face-based presence for residents in an institution or senior residence, including people with severe dementia who repeatedly seek interaction between staff visits. The intended experience is a gentle, friendly stream of conversation and immediate reassurance that can occupy and soothe the resident; it is not a memory test or a replacement for staff. The avatar can acknowledge the resident and invite them to talk about what they want in that moment.
- The tablet could initiate or sustain simple conversation about low-stakes everyday subjects such as food, weather, familiar people, music, and what is happening in the day. Repetition can be part of the reassurance experience rather than treated as a failure of conversation.
- Let the caregiver choose and preview the companion's voice and conversational manner: for example, a male- or female-sounding voice, a familiar accent such as Canadian or British English, pitch, speaking pace, and a calm, cheerful, or more matter-of-fact delivery. The available voices and controls depend on the selected text-to-speech engine and whether its voice packs are installed; wording and script style also help create the intended attitude.
- A family member's recorded voice messages, greetings, jokes, or stories, available on demand or at a chosen time.
- A conversational companion that can gracefully handle pauses, repeated questions, a change of subject, or a wish to stop.
- Companion identity and framing remain open. A warm, friend-like voice may feel more natural than technical language such as “AI”; a simple introduction such as “I'm Mabel, calling for a chat” is one option.
- A fictional “old neighborhood friend” or childhood-friend framing could be explored as an individual, optional approach because it may feel familiar and welcoming to some people. The family or care team could choose a framing that seems comforting for that person, then observe how it lands and revise it if needed.
- Family-configured compassionate responses are another possibility when a literal answer may repeatedly cause grief. For example, if the resident asks about a deceased loved one, a trusted family member might prefer a reassuring, gentle answer or redirection rather than making the person relive the death each time. The response should be chosen for that person and situation, not imposed as a universal rule.
- The app could let family choose and review a response style for recurring sensitive questions: direct answer, reassurance, reminiscence, or gentle redirection. Avoid inventing elaborate claims; prioritize the person's comfort, dignity, and the family's knowledge of what tends to help.
- Compare different companion framings rather than treating one answer as universal. A family may prefer not to have the system pose as a specific child; another familiar, friend-like framing may feel more natural to their relative. Keep the choice person-specific and revisable.
- A shared family story collection built from photos, music, and short audio clips, for the person to explore with someone else or alone.
- A “call someone I love” screen with a few large, familiar contacts.

### Possible extensions to the conversation helper

#### Scripted and local-first extensions

- Family setup for the resident's name, preferred greeting, family names, interests, familiar places, favorite music, and topics to avoid.
- A family review screen to preview and edit scripts before loading them for the resident.
- Reusable conversation templates with varied openings, follow-up questions, and endings, so calls have variety without generating every call live.
- Topic packs that family can add or remove, such as grandchildren, pets, music, gardening, hometown memories, or the current season.
- Adjustable call schedule, ring volume, voice speed, pause length, conversation length, quiet hours, and snooze/skip controls.
- A very simple call screen with large “answer,” “later,” and “stop” controls; optionally start after the person picks up or taps once.
- An easy way to repeat a line, move to the next topic, or finish the call early.
- Better turn-taking: wait through thinking pauses, avoid talking over the resident, and use a gentle neutral fallback when the script cannot respond to what was said.
- Optional limited branching from on-device speech recognition or simple answers; the basic version can remain speech-and-pause detection only, with no attempt to understand the words.
- A small local record of which calls were answered or skipped, if useful to family and acceptable to the resident; avoid recording or storing the conversation by default.
- Offline updates to the phone's script library, such as importing a family-reviewed content pack without creating an ongoing cloud account.
- A power-outage companionship mode: if the phone still has battery, it can offer a familiar offline conversation when internet or household power appears to be unavailable, helping make a quiet, unsettling moment feel less lonely. A rotary-phone/Raspberry Pi version would need its own backup power to work during an outage.
- An outage conversation pack with calm, familiar topics and a gentle offer to contact family. The app should describe only what it actually knows (for example, that its internet connection is unavailable) and avoid claiming that the home has lost power unless that is reliably established.
- Scheduled companion calls should continue from the phone's local schedule and offline script library if Wi-Fi, internet, or a cloud service drops; connectivity loss should not silently cancel the next planned conversation.
- Family content setup could use a simple guided form for approved names, relationships, interests, and topics to avoid, instead of asking families to manage raw files by hand.
- During setup, invite family to optionally identify topics or people to avoid, including a loved one who has died, an estranged or difficult relationship, or a subject connected with grief. Do not assume that asking about children, partners, or pets will be comforting.
- Keep “topics to avoid” separate from general background details: a person or event being in the profile should not automatically make it appropriate for the companion to mention it.
- Let family preview, edit, approve, replace, and remove each generated conversation before it reaches the resident; show when a script was last reviewed so outdated details can be refreshed.
- Mark facts that can change (such as where a relative lives, a pet's status, or an upcoming visit) for review, and make it easy to remove a topic that is no longer welcome.
- Organize conversations by topic, length, or gentle mood, and rotate through them to reduce repetition without needing to analyze the resident's speech.
- Offer adjustable voice, pronunciation of names, speaking pace, volume, and pauses; let the family hear a preview and the resident choose what feels comfortable.
- Keep the resident in control: an easy way to decline, ask for a repeat, move on, or end the call early, plus a quiet-hours setting and a way to change the call schedule.
- Include a kind recovery line for unexpected responses, silence, or unclear speech; do not quiz, correct, or imply that the app understood more than it did.
- For an offline script to respond specifically to a question about a deceased loved one, it would need a prewritten branch and some way to recognize that topic (such as limited local speech recognition or a caregiver-selected conversation path); simple speech/pause detection alone cannot tell what the resident asked.
- Keep the script library transparent and reviewable: show which family-provided details a conversation uses, and avoid storing call audio or transcripts unless there is a clear, agreed reason.
- If the app keeps any local call history, make it minimal (for example, answered or skipped) and separate it from conversation content; do not turn call activity into a mood or health score.
- An optional adaptive conversation profile for cloud-enabled use: the service could notice clear preference corrections and update a small topic profile. For example, if the resident repeatedly says they no longer like hockey, the system could stop choosing hockey scripts and suggest removing that topic from the family profile.
- Instead of retaining full transcripts, the service could keep only reviewed preference signals (for example, “avoid hockey” or “enjoyed talking about gardening”) and show the family a short bullet list of proposed updates.
- Treat preference signals as sensitive and potentially mistaken: make this opt-in, explain what is inferred and who sees it, show the resident's own wording or a brief reason where appropriate, and let the family correct or delete the update before it changes future scripts.
- Keep adaptation descriptive and limited to conversation preferences, not clinical or mood scoring. Avoid treating one response as a stable preference; distinguish a clear correction from a guess based on silence or changing the subject.
- This is a later, more complex option than basic speech/pause detection: the service needs to interpret speech, but it need not keep a full transcript or raw audio after extracting an approved preference update.

#### Possible cloud conversational-avatar setup

- The tablet app provides the resident-facing experience: a large “Talk now” control, microphone and speaker handling during a conversation, the animated face, captions or simple visual cues if useful, and easy pause/stop controls.
- The avatar could be built at several levels: a few prerecorded video expressions, a layered 2D illustrated face whose eyes and mouth are animated, or a rigged 3D character. A simple 2D face is a possible first prototype; it can blink, look toward the resident, switch between listening and speaking, and move its mouth while talking.
- OpenGL ES is one option for drawing custom 2D/3D graphics, but it is a low-level graphics API, not an animation system by itself. A basic Android prototype could use normal app drawing and animation tools; a detailed 3D face would need a 3D model/animation setup and a rendering engine or graphics API.
- Mouth movement can start with a simple open/close animation that follows the volume of the spoken audio. More detailed lip sync uses speech timing or phoneme-to-mouth-shape cues (often called visemes) from the text-to-speech output. The avatar can animate locally on the tablet while the voice is generated in the cloud.
- A simple hand-drawn mouth animation could use five or six aligned transparent mouth images (for example closed, slightly open, open, wide, and rounded). The app can switch among them or briefly cross-fade between neighboring shapes with a short easing transition. A cross-fade is straightforward but can look ghosted if the two mouth shapes differ a lot; smoothly deforming a vector mouth is another option if more fluid motion is wanted.
- Keep the avatar animation lightweight so it can run on older tablets: favor a simple 2D face and smooth transitions between neighboring mouth shapes over demanding 3D effects.
- Keep avatar assets, animation states, and visual behavior inside the tablet app. The cloud conversation service does not need to send graphics or movement commands; the tablet can animate its own face based on whether it is listening or speaking and on the local speech playback.
- A text-only cloud conversation path is possible if the tablet performs speech recognition locally, sends the recognized text to the cloud, receives reply text, and uses local text-to-speech. This reduces audio transfer and keeps avatar animation entirely local. If speech recognition or voice generation is cloud-based instead, audio still needs to be sent to or received from the service.
- The basic character can face the screen and animate on its own, without a camera tracking the resident. Camera-based gaze or presence detection is a separate optional feature, not a requirement for making the avatar feel alive.
- During an active conversation, the tablet sends the resident's speech securely to a conversation service. A simple cloud flow could convert speech to text, form a reply using the family-approved profile and conversation rules, and convert the reply to speech. The avatar plays the returned audio and animates its mouth in time with the voice.
- A more natural real-time audio service could combine listening, response generation, and speech output, but a staged speech-to-text / reply / text-to-speech flow may be easier to prototype and inspect.
- A caregiver setup page or companion app could manage the resident's profile, language, schedule, topics to include or avoid, preferred response styles, and approved reassuring responses. Those protected settings stay separate from what the resident says during a chat.
- The cloud conversation service should use the family-approved profile as context and stay within the companion role. A resident's conversational request should not be able to change administrator settings, expose credentials, or operate unrelated devices.
- Keep the face animation, basic controls, schedule, and a small set of prepared conversations or radio programs on the tablet. If Wi-Fi or the cloud fails, the app can calmly offer those local options instead of stopping completely.
- An on-demand “Talk now” button can begin a session immediately; scheduled sessions can use the same conversation flow. The tablet need not stream continuously while idle: listening can begin only after the resident chooses to chat or an agreed session starts.
- Camera-based presence or gaze detection could be considered later, but it is optional to the basic voice-chat design. If used, it adds sensitive data and should have a clear purpose and a local-processing option where feasible.
- Keep the data path and retention choices explicit: encrypt the connection, limit what profile details are sent, avoid saving raw audio or full transcripts by default, and make any family summaries or preference updates opt-in and reviewable.
- Possible build stages: (1) animated face with prerecorded conversation and family-selected prompts; (2) cloud speech recognition and generated spoken replies; (3) richer real-time voice, optional preference adaptation, and remote caregiver tools. Keep offline content usable at each stage.

#### Possible setup journey for family or care staff

- A trusted family member or care staff member installs the app on the tablet and chooses a private setup area, separate from the resident-facing companion screen.
- Use an interactive setup interview instead of one large form: ask one clear question at a time, explain briefly how the answer will be used, and let the setup person skip a question, come back later, or edit answers.
- Example prompts could ask what name and pronunciation the resident prefers, which languages and voices feel comfortable, what everyday topics or activities they enjoy, which familiar people or places are welcome conversation topics, what tends to reassure them, and which topics or response styles should be avoided.
- The setup person can also describe the resident's day-to-day life and preferences—such as whether they have a dog, enjoy going to the park, play bridge or bingo, and what regular activities, routines, or reminder times belong in the schedule.
- The interview could also ask what the companion should do when the resident asks for a repeated need or seems worried—for example, which family-approved reassuring line or gentle change of subject usually helps. Answers should be reviewed and adjustable before they shape any scripts.
- The setup interview itself could be an AI-guided conversation with the family member or care staff: it asks about the resident's life and preferences, follows up when useful, and turns the answers into an organized profile and draft conversation library. The AI is helping the administrator prepare the service, not interviewing the resident in this setup step.
- A script-generation service such as ChatGPT could use the approved profile to draft varied conversations, response branches for repeated questions, and offline fallback responses for loss of internet or cloud access. The app downloads and stores the family-reviewed scripts on the tablet so it can continue without a live AI connection.
- Generated content should be a reviewed script library, not executable instructions. The setup person can preview, edit, approve, or remove drafts before importing them; the library can include language-specific scripts and offline versions.
- They choose the resident's preferred name, language, voice, accent, speaking pace, and a simple companion style. The setup can offer sample voices for preview; the resident can take part in these choices as much as they want and are able.
- They add only useful conversation details: familiar people and places, interests, favorite activities, pronunciation notes, and topics or people to avoid. They choose how the companion should respond to recurring sensitive questions, such as with reassurance, a familiar topic, or a gentle change of subject.
- They select the experience modes: on-demand “Talk now,” scheduled check-ins, offline friendly radio, or a mix. They set timing, volume, quiet periods, and how long a conversation should last.
- They review and preview example conversations, voice, and avatar behavior before enabling them. They can edit or remove content that sounds awkward, feels repetitive, or is no longer suitable.
- They select whether conversations use the local-only path or optional cloud features, review what data leaves the tablet and what records are kept, and set who has administrator access.
- They place the tablet where it is easy to see and hear, then test it with the resident or care staff. The family or care team can refine the voice, topics, schedule, and reassurance style based on how the resident responds.
- Ongoing maintenance should be simple: update changing details, remove topics that are no longer welcome, and review optional summaries or preference suggestions without needing to manage raw files.
- The setup person could use a web UI on their own desktop computer to enter or edit recurring schedules, dates, times, reminder wording, and offline behavior. The setup and resident profile do not have to be stored on an external server. Examples include a morning wake-up prompt, a medication reminder at a prescribed time, dog-walk reminders, taking out the garbage on Wednesday evening, and one-time appointments.
- Preferred local-first transfer idea: the caregiver's desktop web UI sends the approved schedule, prompts, and offline scripts directly to the resident's phone over the local Wi-Fi network, with no central server holding the content. A simpler first version could export an encrypted configuration/content bundle for import into the phone app; the phone stores and runs the active schedule and scripts locally.
- A future optional cloud mode could support remote profile updates when caregiver and resident are not on the same network, but local setup and offline use should remain possible without an external service.
- Keep updates understandable and manageable: show the caregiver what will change, let them review and confirm an update, and make it clear when the resident's phone has received it. Schedules and imported scripts are data, not executable commands.

Android can report network connectivity changes; access to Wi-Fi identifiers such as the SSID can have additional permission requirements.
- Wi-Fi changes may still be useful as contextual evidence alongside a caregiver-entered routine. For example, if home Wi-Fi is lost and restored around the usual morning dog-walk time on several days, the report could say “home Wi-Fi connection changed around the planned dog-walk time on three days.” This is a pattern that may support a caregiver's interpretation, not confirmation that the person or dog went out; allow caregivers to confirm, correct, or dismiss it.
- Explore a minimal, local-first morning activity summary using signals already available from the phone, without GPS, camera, or continuous microphone listening: first phone motion after a quiet overnight period; first screen interaction or unlock; unplugging from overnight charging; and home Wi-Fi connection changes. These can indicate device activity or a change in connectivity, not prove that the resident woke, got out of bed, or completed a planned activity. A caregiver-approved spoken check-in can add an explicit response; step estimates are a possible optional addition if the phone is usually carried. Keep records coarse and show the source of each signal.
- Combine passive observations and app actions into a simple timeline so the caregiver can interpret them in context—for example, “phone motion detected at 7:45 a.m.; unplugged from power at 8:00 a.m.; home Wi-Fi connection changed at 8:12 a.m.” If this matches the resident's usual supermarket outing, it may suggest the phone was picked up and taken along; without location data or confirmation, the app should leave the destination as the caregiver's inference. Also record when the app itself sent a reminder (for example, a 3:00 p.m. medication prompt), but keep that separate from whether the resident responded or completed the activity.
- Other low-detail phone signals to explore for an opt-in caregiver summary include first phone interaction after a quiet period, screen unlock/use times, charging or unplugging events, and step or movement estimates when supported. These describe phone/device activity and can be wrong or incomplete; avoid collecting message contents, browsing history, or unrelated app usage for this purpose.
- Ambient microphone listening to infer when the resident wakes up is a possible but technically and privacy-sensitive idea. On Android, background microphone access is restricted and microphone use must be apparent to the user; a scheduled morning prompt with a simple “I'm up” response, or a first-use/activity signal, may be a more practical starting point. Ambient sound can also come from a television, another person, or a pet, so it would only be an uncertain signal.
- A broader morning activity check could be useful even if the resident never picks up the phone: for example, a caregiver may want to know that there was activity after the usual wake time, or that television-like audio was detected. This is ambient home monitoring rather than ordinary phone-app behavior; a dedicated local hub or selected room sensors may be more suitable than a phone app for scheduled sensing. Treat “activity detected” or “TV-like sound detected” as observations, not proof that the resident is awake or watching TV.
- For this monitoring use case, assume setup is handled by the caregiver or other person responsible for the resident's phone/home system, with access to configure its security and privacy settings, select which signals are monitored, and receive the resulting caregiver reports.
- A bounded ambient check could run at a caregiver-selected time—such as 8:00 a.m. for someone who usually wakes around 7:30—and sample sound for a short period, perhaps 30 seconds. It could send a brief caregiver update such as “TV-like sound detected” or “sound activity detected.” Explore doing the classification locally and discarding the audio immediately, without keeping a recording. A dedicated home hub or sensor is likely a better host for this scheduled background check than a standard Android phone app.
- Consider an optional caregiver-requested live audio check-in/intercom between the caregiver's app and the resident's phone over Wi-Fi or mobile data, rather than the cellular phone network. In the normal flow, the resident's phone clearly announces the incoming session and who is asking; the resident can answer or decline and can end it at any time. Use an authenticated, end-to-end encrypted audio stream, show a clear active-microphone indicator, limit session duration, and do not record by default; keep an access record for the caregiver.
- Offer a separate one-way caregiver voice message: the caregiver records or selects a short audio clip in their app, and the resident's phone announces who sent it, then plays it aloud through the speaker automatically—no unlocking, password, inbox, or accept button is required (for example, “Hi Mom, it's Alex. I tried calling and wanted to check that you're okay. Can you pick up the phone?”). A message can also give a simple follow-up reminder, such as “Please call Andrew this afternoon to let him know you're okay.” The resident's microphone stays off; this is playback, not remote listening. After playback, offer an optional “Accept to talk” action; only after the resident accepts does the app open a two-way intercom and activate the microphone. The resident can also stop or dismiss the message without accepting. Only authenticated, explicitly linked caregivers can send messages; protect delivery in transit and at rest, and do not retain message audio longer than needed unless the caregiver explicitly chooses to save it. Verify background playback and interruption behavior on target phone platforms. During setup, test audibility and explain that the app cannot guarantee playback over a user-muted volume setting or Do Not Disturb; Android users control notification-channel behavior, and volume changes that affect Do Not Disturb require special access. A message may play when the resident is in another room or otherwise unable to hear; report only delivery or playback status where available, never assume it was heard.
- Keep caregiver voice messages and two-way intercom audio optimized for intelligible speech rather than music-grade fidelity: mono, speech-oriented lossy compression, low latency, and adaptive bitrate to fit weak mobile connections and reduce data use. Intercom is a short, explicitly started session with a clear end—not continuous or 24-hour audio. Test that voices remain understandable on older phones and ordinary speakers.
- Explore a separate emergency check-in path for the case where the caregiver has tried calling and the resident does not answer, possibly because they are incapacitated. Do not assume non-response proves an emergency. Android's microphone permission is subject to while-in-use restrictions: an ordinary app generally cannot start microphone capture remotely while it is in the background. A notification/request that the resident answers, or a session already started visibly on the resident's phone, is more feasible; a silent remote listen-in must not be promised. If the resident cannot respond, the app can show the caregiver recent device/location signals and escalate to another chosen contact or emergency services according to the caregiver's plan. Any future pre-authorized, automatically activated listen-in would need platform-specific feasibility work, clear disclosure and visible indication, tight duration and scope, encryption, no recording by default, and explicit setup consent.

## Wandering and lost-person support

- Explore a caregiver-configured map of the resident's familiar life: the small set of places they visit routinely (home, their usual supermarket, bank, park, friends' homes) and the usual walking, driving, or bus corridors connecting them. The intended coverage is the places and routes where the resident spends nearly all of their time (the caregiver's estimate might be “about 99%”), with room to add a one-off destination such as an appointment. Normal movement within a familiar place or route should not trigger an alert.
- Frame this for caregivers as passive monitoring of familiar day-to-day activities around the person's usual locations and travel corridors. It stays quiet while the phone follows that familiar pattern; if it appears to leave the usual route corridor or move unexpectedly far from mapped places—for example, someone gets off the bus on the way home and continues away from home—the app could initiate a gentle, brief voice check-in: “Hi Margaret, is everything okay? Do you need help?” Then offer directions to a chosen familiar place or a call to the caregiver. The caregiver sets places, route corridors, distance/time tolerances, and alert preferences.
- Let caregivers map multiple familiar alternatives between places (for example, several walking routes or bus paths) and set a distance buffer around each corridor. A small detour, GPS variation, or temporary bus diversion within the buffer should not count as leaving the familiar pattern. Escalation should consider how far outside the nearest familiar corridor the phone is, how long it stays there, whether it is continuing farther away, and the reported location accuracy—not just whether it crossed a route line.
- Use a configurable escalation sequence for unusual location patterns: first send the caregiver an update describing the observed event and time, leaving them to decide whether to call, wait, or take another action. If the phone continues moving farther outside the familiar pattern and the caregiver alert has not been acknowledged in the app after a chosen interval, optionally trigger a gentle simulated call to the resident: “Hi, do you need help?” Let the resident ask for directions or a call to someone. An unacknowledged app alert does not prove the caregiver is unavailable, so make the delay and escalation choice configurable, avoid repeated calls, and offer the caregiver a way to cancel or pause escalation.
- Treat location patterns as signals that can trigger a check-in, not as certainty about the person's intention or safety. GPS drift, an alternate route, a changed destination, or delayed/missing background updates can resemble wandering. Use a configurable distance/time threshold to avoid reacting to small location jumps; give the person time to answer, avoid repeated or alarming calls, and offer a caregiver contact if help is requested or the situation remains unclear. Android background location requires explicit permission and geofence events may be delayed, so do not promise real-time rescue or make this the only safety measure.
- Add a simple voice-first “I need help getting home” action on the resident's phone. It could call a chosen caregiver, share the phone's current location if enabled, or speak a family-configured contact instruction. Keep the interaction very simple and make clear when a location has or has not been sent.
- Consider a durable ID card, medical ID, or wearable with a caregiver contact number as a companion to phone-based features, since the resident may leave the phone behind, run out of battery, or lose connectivity.
- A lost-person support page for the caregiver could keep a current photo, important identifying details, likely destinations, and the agreed response contacts together for quick access. Keep this information private and locally stored where possible.
- Treat a reported missing-person event (for example, an active Silver Alert) as an explicit emergency exception to ordinary access: authorized law enforcement or responders should be able to access location information needed to find the resident, including current or recent movement where available. The product should support more than caregiver-initiated sharing if a verified emergency request is the appropriate route in that situation.
- Design a rapid, verified, incident-scoped responder access flow: define how the emergency and requesting authority are authenticated, what location window is available, how live tracking or recent history is provided, and how access is logged and ends when the incident closes or a set time expires. The exact request/authorization process can be settled with relevant responders and legal/privacy specialists for each launch jurisdiction.
- Make any pre-authorized responder-access feature explicitly opt-in during caregiver setup for each resident, with a plain-language choice to enable or decline it. Explain which verified responders could access what location data, under what missing-person/emergency conditions, for how long, and how access is logged. Keep the setting reviewable and changeable; do not enable responder access by default.
- Keep emergency access distinct from ordinary access: no routine or standing police access to residents' histories, and no universal hidden key. End-to-end encryption means a relay service cannot produce readable location data unless an authorized endpoint or deliberately designed responder mechanism can decrypt it. Resolve this emergency-access/key design before promising both E2EE and independent responder access.
- Possible emergency-access models to compare: (1) the caregiver deliberately shares a time-limited location/history package from their device; (2) a verified responder becomes an additional, incident-limited recipient, with the resident's device encrypting only the emergency data to that responder's public key; or (3) a prearranged key-escrow/threshold recovery mechanism. The first is simplest but depends on caregiver availability; the second needs responder identity/key infrastructure; the third adds a high-value key and greater trust risk. In every model, responders are explicit recipients for that incident, access is scoped and audited, and there is no blanket decryption key.
- For the responder-recipient model, a time limit can stop future location updates and close the responder's active session, but it cannot cryptographically make a recipient forget or erase information they already decrypted or copied. Make the time window, downloadable history, and retention expectations explicit.
- Apply least access to emergency responders: for a verified missing-person event (such as a Silver Alert), the responder-facing KinBridge view should provide only a small recent location slice needed to find the person—for example, the latest five or six GPS points with timestamps and accuracy, or a caregiver/responder-authorized bounded window from that day, plus optional time-limited live updates. It should not expose scripts, daily routines, future schedules, full location history, caregiver reports, or the caregiver's account. Keep the broader profile and history available only to the resident and authorized caregiver(s); handle any broader legal demand through the applicable jurisdiction's process rather than expanding routine responder access.
- Treat phone tracking as one part of a broader, caregiver-planned safety approach, not a guarantee that a missing person can be located. Explore which features suit the resident's circumstances and preserve a simple path to human help.

## Questions and guardrails to keep in view

- Start from moments in an ordinary day: waking, getting ready, meals, time alone, going out, and bedtime. Notice what is hard, enjoyable, confusing, or reassuring.
- Keep the person's comfort and preferences central. The person can decline, pause, or end an interaction; family- or care-team-configured reassurance styles and topic choices can be tailored to what appears to help that individual.
- Treat voice, camera, location, family details, and routine data as sensitive. Explore what can be avoided, processed locally, or shared only with permission.
- Decide how the companion introduces itself and whether a familiar persona is appropriate for a particular person. This remains an open, family-configurable design question, not a universal rule.
- A possible fall alert or routine observation can be wrong. Any safety-oriented idea needs a real person in the loop and should not be the sole emergency plan.
- A reminder or sensor event cannot necessarily establish that someone completed an action, such as taking medication. Reports should distinguish app outputs (reminder sent), resident responses (if any), passive observations (such as phone motion or a pillbox opening), and confirmed completion; do not label a dose as taken based on the reminder alone.
- Explore ideas with the person, family, and caregivers before assuming what would be welcome.
- No idea is selected yet; keep collecting possibilities before narrowing the project.
